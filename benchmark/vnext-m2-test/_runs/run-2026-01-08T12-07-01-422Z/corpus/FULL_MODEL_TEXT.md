# Designing Microlearning That Actually Sticks

## Context
A team wants to turn internal know-how into short learning units for new managers. They keep producing “nice-to-read” pages, but managers still fail in real conversations. The team assumes the problem is motivation. A coach argues the real problem is *interference*: people freeze under pressure, forget the first step, or choose the wrong tool because they misunderstand what the tool is for.

This chapter introduces a small toolbox you can reuse across topics.

## The Outcome Ladder
When someone says “I want to learn coaching,” ask what success looks like in the real world. The Outcome Ladder separates outcomes into four rungs:

Rung A: remember (can recall terms)  
Rung B: recognize (can pick the right option from examples)  
Rung C: perform (can do the behavior in a realistic scenario)  
Rung D: transfer (can do it later, in a different context, under stress)

A common mistake is to test only Rung A and celebrate progress while performance stays flat.

## The Friction Budget
Every learning unit has a friction budget: the maximum mental effort a learner can spend before they quit or start guessing. Friction grows when the unit requires too many new terms at once, too many steps, or too much switching between screens. If the friction budget is exceeded, learners stop doing deliberate practice and default to superficial reading.

This does not mean “make everything easy.” It means you spend friction where it buys transfer.

## The 7-Minute Microlearning Loop
Use this loop to design one unit:

Step 1: Choose one Outcome Ladder rung as the target (A, B, C, or D).  
Step 2: Write one “observable pass condition” (what you can see or measure).  
Step 3: Create one short scenario with a constraint (time pressure, an annoyed colleague, missing data).  
Step 4: Offer two actions: the intended action and a tempting wrong action.  
Step 5: Ask for a choice *and* a one-sentence reason.  
Step 6: Give feedback in two layers: first a short rule of thumb, then a deeper explanation.  
Step 7: Add a “next repetition” prompt: when and how to repeat (tomorrow, next meeting, or next real case).

## Two-Speed Feedback
Feedback should come in two speeds. The fast layer is a rule of thumb the learner can remember during a live conversation. The slow layer explains why the rule works and when it fails. The fast layer prevents overthinking; the slow layer prevents blind habit.

# Two-Speed Feedback

## “More content means more learning”
Teams often believe that adding more examples and more theory increases learning. In reality, extra content can raise friction without improving transfer. If you need three paragraphs to justify a rule of thumb, your unit is probably targeting the wrong rung or mixing multiple skills.

## “If they understood it once, they will perform it later”
Recognition is not performance. People can nod at a good model and still fail under stress. Transfer requires repetition spaced over time and practice under constraints.

## The Calibration Loop
A learner calibrates by comparing their choice to an expert choice and understanding the difference. Calibration improves fastest when the learner must commit to a decision before seeing the answer, and when the feedback names the hidden variable (for example: risk, trust, time, power, or uncertainty).

## Prefer Fewer, Stronger Links
A note system should connect ideas so that a learner (or author) can move from one note to the next without reading everything. But linking every noun creates noise. Links should point to stable concepts, named models, or repeated prerequisites. Everything else can be left as plain text until it proves useful.

## A sample scenario
You are a manager. A team member says: “I keep missing deadlines because other teams block me.” You have two actions:

Action A: Ask for a concrete recent example and explore constraints before proposing a solution.  
Action B: Give advice immediately: “Just communicate earlier and set boundaries.”

Many new managers choose B because it feels helpful. But B often skips the diagnostic step and can trigger defensiveness.

## Tip
The toolbox above works best when the skill can be observed (even roughly). It can fail when outcomes are invisible or when the environment blocks practice (for example: no permission to try, high risk, or strict scripts).

## Ask yourself  
1) How should you adapt the Friction Budget for expert learners who get bored easily?  
2) How do you measure transfer when the real-world event happens only once a month?
